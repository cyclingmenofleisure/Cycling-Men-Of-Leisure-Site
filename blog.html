S5E45 · October 2, 2026

Darth Vader Walks Into Buffalo Trace With A Ghost Meter

Spooky season is back by popular demand, and this year the Halloween special is a two-parter. Michael opens with a riddle — what do Freddie Johnson of Buffalo Trace, the Queen Mary and Darth Vader have in common? — while sipping a seasonal Moonlight Mayhem. The Listener Spotlight reveal is Sarasota, Florida: nobody got it (Adam "cheated" his way to Gatlinburg, Tennessee), and Michael explains the Barbie wardrobes, John Ringling's circus winter quarters, Stephen Root and Milton's red Swingline stapler in Office Space, and the 99% pure quartz sand on Siesta Key that stays cool on hot days.

Then comes trick-or-treat nostalgia. Adam grew up on a rural route outside Anna, Illinois, where trick-or-treating meant driving a mile between houses, and his costumes were all Star Wars: Darth Vader, Luke Skywalker and an Ewok, with a homemade lightsaber his dad built from an Air Force survival flashlight and a fluorescent-bulb tube. That's followed by a side story about an Air Force parachute knife, a principal named Dr. Cobble, and one early step toward military school. Michael went as Casper, Batman, a fur trader and a cowboy, and now lives in a town where Halloween night looks like the scene from E.T.

The centerpiece is the Cycling Men of Leisure list of the 12 most haunted places in the world, counted down Letterman-style: the Ancient Ram Inn, the Myrtles, the Queen Mary, Château de Brissac and its Green Lady, the Stanley Hotel, Mexico's Island of the Dolls, Eastern State Penitentiary, Romania's Hoia-Baciu Forest, Bhangarh Fort, Edinburgh Castle and its vanished piper, Poveglia in the Venetian lagoon (once Adam's AI helps with the pronunciation), and, at number one, the Tower of London. Michael closes with ghost-hunting TV at bourbon distilleries: TAPS at Buffalo Trace, where Freddie Johnson tells of a voice that warned workers out of a barrel house just before the racks came down, plus Wild Turkey and the abandoned Old Crow plant next to Castle & Key, where Glenns Creek now operates.

A new set of Spotlight clues wraps up part one: a tactical army base, royal street plans, a port for labor, a city that pioneered borrowing, and one that loves its bugs. Michael wonders aloud whether it could be St. Louis, and Adam has the whole Italy crew working on it. Part two, with a new chapter of Adam's AI Halloween story, is coming up next.

S5E44 · September 18, 2026

Why A $2,000 Foldable Phone Sparks A Bigger Debate

Recorded a little early so the show keeps its cadence while Adam heads across the pond, this one opens with Michigan football — 2-0 after squeaking past Oklahoma, though Michael isn't buying the officiating or the extra seconds that kept appearing on the clock the week before. There's a friendly hype-video standoff (James Earl Jones for Michigan, Johnny Carson for Nebraska), a primer on the "Mr. Brightside" phone-light show at night games, and a look ahead: seven episodes left in the year, a two-part Halloween special, the Leisure Community Christmas wish list, and the January golf outing around Podfest in Florida.

The Listener Spotlight reveal is Silver Spring, Maryland, and nobody got it — not even Adam, who really should have caught the "moving people" clue about the longest escalator in the Western Hemisphere. Michael walks through the mica spring that gave the town its name, its status as an edge city of Washington, D.C., and the museum that holds the bullet that killed Abraham Lincoln. There's a milestone too: the show just passed 100,000 downloads in the United States alone, and roughly a fifth of listeners now tune in from outside the country.

Then the gear debates. Apple's new foldable iPhone starts at $2,000, and Michael's text pitch was that double the screens means double the bus riders. The guys pick apart the monthly-payment math that makes a two-grand phone feel cheap, and Michael bets Adam will own one — with a review due the first episode of December. Michael counters with Specialized's Built For Legends Limited Collection: 199 S-Works road bikes, 119 cross-country, 55 gravel and 50 downhill, each finished in a red glaze over silver metallic dust with a hand-brushed ink wash so no two frames match, priced from about $11,000 to $15,500. Adam remembers the $14,999 Project One Trek sitting in the corner of the shop when he bought his first road bike in 2013, and explains why he's sticking with the Noble Steed.

The back half covers trends everyday riders feel: gravel getting more serious (Michael's theory is that gravel lovers didn't grow up on country roads), e-bikes becoming everyday transportation, kids riding fast without helmets, lithium battery fires and charging etiquette at events, and a bike industry still finding its balance after the pandemic boom. A fresh set of Spotlight clues closes it out — Barbie-worthy closets, the Swingline stapler, and an official sixth hint: it is not St. Louis.

S5E43 · September 4, 2026

What If Leisure Is The Real Engine Of Labor

A Labor Day episode that starts with a question worth chewing on: what if leisure is the thing that drove labor, and not the other way around? Before the history lesson, though, there's trip prep. Adam and Lauren are eighteen days out from Italy — Rome to Venice by train, a week on a Royal Caribbean sailing around the boot, then Florence, Tuscany, Pisa and three days back in Rome. He fed a 114-page stack of tour itineraries through an LLM and asked it to flag every place the plan could fall apart, and it came back with a dozen pinch points, including the small detail that the cruise doesn't actually end in Rome but 87 kilometers northwest of it. Michael's prediction for the seven-minute train connection at Roma Termini is not optimistic.

The Listener Spotlight is a big one. Bend, Oregon is revealed, and for the first time in a while there are two winners — Melanie Erickson and Armando Rodriguez both nailed it, and Melanie backed it up with a photo of the van parked outside the last Blockbuster on earth. Matt Robinson made a genuinely good case for Grand Rapids, Michigan and came up just short. Elsewhere in community news: Dennis Keeler is in on the January golf outing at PodFest, Tyler Carpenter is back on the bike and riding San Diego to Savannah for the Fuller Center for Housing, and a new subscriber named Terry gets a well-earned thank you.

Then the main event. The first Labor Day was September 5, 1882 — ten thousand workers marching from New York's City Hall to a picnic in Wendel's Elm Park. The guys trace the Haymarket Affair, the Pullman Strike and Grover Cleveland's 1894 signature, why the United States deliberately avoided May 1 (May Day had picked up political baggage abroad), and the long march toward the eight-hour day through the Adamson Act, the Fair Labor Standards Act and Henry Ford's five-day week. The through line is the good part: rank-and-file workers kept trading speeches and picket lines for barbecues and bike races, and the golden age of American velodrome racing landed right in the middle of it. Bicycles helped sell the country on leisure.

Also in the mix: Tadej Pogačar's crash out of the Vuelta a España on stage eight after a dominant opening week, Michigan bicycle crashes down three percent, a two-way cycling track coming to Belle Isle, Team USA's 34-rider squad for the World Championships in Montreal, the return of the Philadelphia Cycling Classic after a decade away, traffic gardens for teaching kids to ride, and Team Amani's push to get East and Central African women to the Tour de France Femmes by 2028. A new set of Spotlight clues closes it out — and Michael admits he's now writing them specifically to stump the AI.
