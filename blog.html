S5E40 · July 27, 2026

Tour De France Finale Breakdown And What It Means Next

The final day of the Tour de France is supposed to be champagne, photos, and a calm roll into Paris. Then the route gets shortened over wildfire security concerns, the temperature climbs, and Tadej Pogacar decides he is not interested in coasting. The Champs-Élysées snapped back into "real race" mode, and it delivered a finale where tactics, pride, and timing mattered just as much as legs.

We walk through how the move formed, why the entire peloton ended up working like a single machine to close it down, and how Mathieu van der Poel turned the last kilometer into a masterclass by hitting the gas at exactly the right moment. Zooming out: Pogacar locks up his fifth Tour de France title at only 27, raising the historical question that won't go away — can he become the first officially recognized six-time winner?

Then there's the podium story that deserves its own spotlight: Isaac del Toro makes history as the first Mexican rider to finish on the Tour de France podium, adding a stage win and the white jersey at 22 while still serving as a loyal teammate. We also get into what made the 2026 Tour feel different — fewer huge crashes than most years, and racing that kept splitting into multiple groups day after day.

To round it out, a quick history hit on why the Champs-Élysées matters, how the Tour's finish moved there in 1975, and a nod to Greg LeMond's unforgettable 1989 finale. What was your favorite moment from Paris?

S5E39 · July 26, 2026

Should You Wait After A Crash Or Just Race

The Tour de France doesn't hand out "hardest day ever" labels lightly, but Stage 20 made the case with brutal climbs, huge elevation, and a finale that punished every lapse in focus. We break down how a record-setting mountain day actually unfolded: why the peloton kept splitting into pieces, how the cold up high changes the feel of the racing, and why the last miles can be more dangerous than the first hundred.

Richard Carapaz takes the stage for EF Education, but the win comes with real late-stage drama. We talk through Sepp Kuss's heartbreaking sequence of two crashes, including a tunnel-to-daylight transition and a costly descent mistake that flipped the outcome in seconds. Meanwhile, Tadej Pogacar rides more defensively and spends energy helping Del Toro up the mountain in a rare, team-first moment that fans will argue about for a long time.

That argument becomes our central question: should a rider ever wait after a rival crashes? We dig into cycling's unwritten rules, when the peloton has historically shown restraint, and why a queen stage finish can change the ethics and the expectations. Then we preview Stage 21, shortened due to wildfires, and ask what that means for the Paris finale on the Champs-Élysées: more aggressive racing, or mostly ceremony with a fast finish?

S5E38 · July 25, 2026

Twenty One Switchbacks And One Absolute Monster

Alpe d'Huez doesn't forgive anyone, and that's exactly why this day hit so hard. A mountain stage that looks short on paper but feels enormous once the climbing begins: the breakaway forms, the chase groups fracture, and with about 10 miles left the yellow jersey sits minutes back. Then Tadej Pogacar hits a gear that makes the gap feel imaginary, picking riders off one by one and turning a messy finale into a clean, terrifying display of climbing control.

We talk through what made the move so decisive, from timing and pacing to how the road itself shapes the racing — Alpe d'Huez's 21 numbered switchbacks create natural reset points and launch pads when everyone is on the limit. We also highlight Adam Yates's bounce-back ride, how a teammate's pull can steady the effort on the steepest ramps, and what it looks like when the best climber in the race treats a legendary ascent like a rehearsal.

There's history baked into every corner here, including Dutch Corner and the way fans transform the mountain into a tunnel of noise. That energy can be electric, but it can also get dangerous — we cover the late crash involving a support vehicle braking to avoid spectators. With the overall win and key jerseys looking close to locked, the real remaining drama: who takes second and third on the podium.
